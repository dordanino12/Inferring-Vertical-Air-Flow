Abstract

Understanding vertical airflow in cloud fields is critical for climate modeling. Anticipating upcoming multi-satellite missions, we propose a framework to infer this airflow both at the visible cloud envelope and targeted internal layers, using sequential passive 2D spaceborne stereo imagery. The estimation is challenging due to high-order scattering, opacity, and violations of brightness constancy. Inference is based on a spatiotemporal deep neural network, trained via a physics-based data generation pipeline. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work to investigate whether spaceborne image sequences contain sufficient indirect information to infer this flow. We show that our learned mapping yields statistically consistent velocity estimates, laying the foundation for processing and exploiting observational data from future satellite space missions.

Overview

Atmospheric studies involve 3D, time-evolving kinematics—especially vertical air velocity and mass flux. Vertical flows serve as a “hidden engine” of deep convection. They largely determine precipitation formation, maximum cloud altitude, storm electrification, and coupling to large-scale climate circulation. The flows are exceptionally difficult to observe. Clouds induce a very high order of scattering, severely degrading visibility to decameters into the cloud envelope. Furthermore, because clouds are translucent and turbulent, brightness constancy over viewpoints and time does not hold. This challenges classic methods of both multi-view computer vision and temporal tracking.

This work proposes solving the problem by a combination of stereoscopic orbiting imaging and machine learning. We further tune the approach towards temporal sequences of upcoming passive spaceborne stereo missions, such as C3IEL (Cluster for Cloud Evolution, Climate and Lightning) and CloudCT. The system infers vertical velocity fields (Vz) both at the visible cloud envelope and across targeted internal horizontal layers.

Physics-based rendering of LES-generated 4D clouds yields synthetic stereo sequences from orbiting satellites, for training. Sequences are processed by a DNN, comprising an encoder, temporal aggregation and a decoder. Outputs are vertical flow velocity fields V̂z(t). The DNN is optimized to fit inference to the ground-truth flow of either the cloud envelope or layer altitudes, Vz(t).

Contributions

  • A fundamentally new problem. This work formulates a fundamentally new problem, and establishes that spatiotemporal stereo sequences contain sufficient indirect signals to retrieve vertical airflow.
  • A 4D cloud evolution and image database. We assembled a unique dataset of volumetric cloud content, flow, and corresponding images from orbit, by physics-based generation: a Large Eddy Simulation of atmospheric fluid dynamics, volumetric path tracing, orbital motion, and solar illumination changes. We introduce and release this database, providing the community with a vital benchmark for future research.

Physics-Based Cloud Data and Rendering

Ground-truth air flow over large cloud fields, with simultaneous images of these clouds from space, does not exist. Therefore, our work relies on physical data generation. We use a Large Eddy Simulation (LES), which outputs rich volumetric spatiotemporal atmospheric fields. Given these physics-based clouds evolving spatiotemporally, corresponding satellite images are rendered using Monte Carlo path tracing, accounting for high-order multiple scattering, orbital motion and solar illumination changes.

Key Results

Typical boundary-layer cloud updrafts may reach up to 7 m/s. Field campaigns deploying in-situ aircraft probes through clouds demonstrate that this physical variability (turbulent standard deviation) reaches up to 1.67 m/s. A satisfactory inference model should yield an error standard deviation bounded well within this natural physical variability, i.e., smaller than 1.6 m/s. Our model maintains an error standard deviation (STD) of 1.02 m/s. Our inference model seems to have a smaller uncertainty than natural variability in a chaotic atmospheric vertical flow.

Real-World Experiment

Until stereo-satellites are launched, there is no suitable data in existence. We found coordinated flights in the 2013 NASA SEAC4RS field campaign: an ER-2 aircraft imaged clouds with AirMSPI from 20 km, while a DC-8 aircraft flew in that general region, yielding 1,317 in-situ velocity measurements. We infer Vz and compare the statistical distributions.

The AirMSPI optical footprint intersects the DC-8 path (green line).

The density distributions are consistent. The Wasserstein distance is 0.15 m/s and the quantile MAE is 0.14 m/s.
